**Problem 1:**

Let $$G = \mathbb{Z}_4\times\mathbb{Z}_4$$ and $$N$$ be the cyclic subgroup generated by $$(3,2)$$.

a) Explicitly write out the subsets of $$G$$ which form the elements of $$G/N$$.
b) Write out an addition table for $$G/N$$.
c) Prove that $$G/N$$ is isomorphic as a group to $$\mathbb{Z}_4$$.

**Solution:**

* a) Let $$H = \langle(3,2)\rangle$$.  By Lagrange, there should be four cosets.  These cosets are

$$\begin{align}
(0,0) + H &= \{(3,2),(2,0),(1,2),(0,0)\},\\
(1,0) + H &= \{(0,2),(3,0),(2,2),(1,0)\},\\
(0,1) + H &= \{(3,3),(2,1),(1,3),(0,1)\},\\
(1,1) + H &= \{(0,3),(3,1),(2,3),(1,1)\}
\end{align}$$

* b) The addition table is

$$\begin{array}{c|cccc}
           & (0,0) + H & (1,0) + H & (0,1) + H & (1,1) + H\\\hline
(0,0) + H  & (0,0) + H & (1,0) + H & (0,1) + H & (1,1) + H\\
(1,0) + H  & (1,0) + H & (0,0) + H & (1,1) + H & (0,1) + H\\
(0,1) + H  & (0,1) + H & (1,1) + H & (1,0) + H & (0,0) + H\\
(1,1) + H  & (1,1) + H & (0,1) + H & (0,0) + H & (1,0) + H
\end{array}$$

* c) Since $$G/H$$ is a group of order $$4$$, it is either isomorphic to $$\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb Z_2$$ or $$\mathbb Z_4$$.
If it were isomorphic to $$\mathbb{Z}_2\times\mathbb Z_2$$, then every element would be it's own inverse.
However, the addition table shows this isn't the case so the group must be $$\mathbb Z_4$$

**Problem 2:**

Consider the subset $$N$$ of $$S_4$$ defined by

$$N = \{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}.$$

a) Prove that $$N$$ is a normal subgroup of $$S_4$$
b) Explicitly describe the cosets of $$S_4/N$$
c) Write out a multiplication table for the quotient group $$S_4/N$$.

**Solution:**

* a) We know from a previous homework that if $$\sigma,\tau\in S_n$$, then $$\tau$$ and $$\sigma \tau\sigma^{-1}$$ both have the same cycle type.
If $$\tau\in N$$ is the identity, then $$\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1} = \sigma e\sigma^{-1} = e = \tau\in N$$ for all $$\sigma\in S_4$$.
Otherwise, if $$\tau\in N$$ is not the identity, then for any $$\sigma\in S_4$$ we know $$\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}$$ has cycle type $$2,2$$.
The set $$N$$ consists of the identity and ALL of the permutations of cycle type $$2,2$$, so $$\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}\in N$$.
Hence $$\sigma N\sigma^{-1}\subseteq N$$, and it follows that $$\sigma N\sigma^{-1} = N$$.
Thus $$N$$ is a normal subgroup of $$S_4$$.
* b) By Lagrange, there should be $$6$$ cosets.  These cosets are

$$\begin{align}
eN    & = \{e,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}\\
(12)N & = \{(12),(34), (1324), (1423)\}\\
(13)N & = \{(13),(1234),(24),(1432)\}\\
(14)N & = \{(14),(1243),(1342),(23)\}\\
(123)N & = \{(123),(134),(243),(142)\}\\
(124)N & = \{(124),(143),(132),(234)\}
\end{align}$$

* c)

$$\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
         & eN     & (12)N  & (13)N  & (14)N  & (123)N & (124)N\\\hline
eN       & eN     & (12)N  & (13)N  & (14)N  & (123)N & (124)N\\
(12)N    & (12)N  &   eN   & (124)N & (123)N &  (14)N & (13)N\\
(13)N    & (13)N  & (123)N &   eN   & (124)N &  (12)N & (14)N\\
(14)N    & (14)N  & (124)N & (123)N &   eN   &  (13)N & (12)N\\
(123)N   & (123)N & (13)N  & (14)N  & (12)N  & (132)N &  eN\\
(124)N   & (124)N & (14)N  & (12)N  & (13)N  &   eN   & (123)N
\end{array}$$


**Problem 3:** 

Suppose that $$\varphi: G\rightarrow H$$ are groups and that $$N$$ is a normal subgroup of $$G$$.

Consider the function

$$\overline{\varphi}: G/N\rightarrow H,\quad g\ast N\mapsto \varphi(g).$$

This function may or may not be well-defined.  When it is, we say $$\varphi$$ **descends to the quotient**.

a) Suppose $$G=\mathbb{R}^3$$, $$H = \mathbb{R}^2$$, 

$$N=\text{span}\left\lbrace\left[\begin{array}{c}1 \\ 1 \\ 1\end{array}\right]\right\rbrace,$$

and let $$\varphi: G\rightarrow H$$ be the linear transformation defined by

$$\varphi:
\left[\begin{array}{c}x \\ y \\ z\end{array}\right]\mapsto
\left[\begin{array}{c}
x + y - 2z\\
2x + 2y -4z
\end{array}\right]$$

Show that $$\overline{\varphi}$$ well-defined.

b) Suppose $$G$$, $$H$$, and $$\varphi$$ are the same as before, but 

$$N=\text{span}\left\lbrace\left[\begin{array}{c}1 \\ 0 & 1\\\end{array}\right]\right\rbrace,$$

Prove that $$\overline{\varphi}$$ is no longer well-defined.

c) Prove in general that if $$N\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$$, then $$\overline{\varphi}$$ is well-defined.


**Solution:**

* a) For $$\overline\varphi$$ to be well-defined, we need to show  $$\varphi(\vec u + N) = \varphi(\vec v + N)$$ whenever $$\vec u + N = \vec v + N$$.  Suppose that

$$\left[\begin{array}{c}x \\ y \\ z\end{array}\right] + N = \left[\begin{array}{c}a \\ b \\ c\end{array}\right] + N.$$

Then there exists $$d\in \mathbb R$$ with

$$\left[\begin{array}{c}x \\ y \\ z\end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c}a + d \\ b + d\\ c + d\end{array}\right].$$

Consequently

$$
\varphi\left(\left[\begin{array}{c}x \\ y \\ z\end{array}\right]\right) = \varphi\left(\left[\begin{array}{c}a + d \\ b + d\\ c + d\end{array}\right]\right) = \left[\begin{array}{c}
(x+d) + (y+d) - 2(z+d)\\
2(x+d) + 2(y+d) -4(z+d)
\end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c}
a + b - 2c\\
2a + 2b -4c
\end{array}\right] = \varphi\left(\left[\begin{array}{c}a \\ b \\ c\end{array}\right]\right).$$

* b) To show that $$\overline\varphi$$ is not well-defined, we need to that there exists $$\vec u$$ and $$\vec v$$ with $$\varphi(\vec u + N) \neq \varphi(\vec v + N)$$ even though $$\vec u + N = \vec v + N$$.  Note that

$$\left[\begin{array}{c}0 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{array}\right] + N = \left[\begin{array}{c}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{array}\right] + N.$$

Also

$$\varphi\left(\left[\begin{array}{c}0 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{array}\right]\right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} 0\\0\end{array}\right] \neq \left[\begin{array}{c} -1\\-2\end{array}\right] = \varphi\left(\left[\begin{array}{c}1 \\ 0 \\ 1\end{array}\right]\right),$$ 

so the function $$\overline\varphi$$ is not well-defined.

* c) Suppose that $$\varphi: G\rightarrow H$$ is a group homomorphism and that $$N$$ is a normal subgroup of $$G$$ with $$N\subseteq \ker(\varphi)$$.
If $$aN = bN$$, then $$a=b\ast n$$ for some $$n\in N$$ and therefore 

$$\overline\varphi(aN) = \varphi(a) = \varphi(b\ast_G n) = \varphi(b)\ast_H \varphi(n) = \varphi(b)\ast_H e_H = \varphi(b) = \overline\varphi(bN).$$

This shows that $$\overline\varphi$$ is well-defined.

**Problem 4:**

Let $$\mathcal M$$ be the Mobius group from Homework 1.
Consider the function

$$\psi: \text{GL}_2(\mathbb C)\rightarrow \mathcal M,\quad
\psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc}
a & b\\ c & d
\end{array}\right]\right) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}.$$

a) Prove that $$\psi$$ is a group epimorphism
b) Prove that $$\mathcal M$$ is isomorphic to the projective general linear group
c) Determine the value of $$\chi^{2025}(1)$$, where $$\chi(z) = \frac{3z-1}{-z+3}$$.  [Hint: matrix diagonalization!]

$$\text{PSL}_2(\mathbb C) = \text{GL}_2(\mathbb C)/N,$$

where here $$N$$ is the normal subgroup of $$\text{GL}_2(\mathbb C)$$ consisting of all scalar multiples of the identity matrix.

**Solution:**

* a) It is clear that $\psi$ is surjective, so we need only show that it is a homomorphism.
We calculate

$$
\begin{align}
 \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} a & b\\ c & d \end{array}\right]\right)
 \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} a' & b'\\ c' & d' \end{array}\right]\right)\\
  & = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}\circ \frac{a'z + b'}{c'z + d'}\\
  & = \frac{a\left(\frac{a'z + b'}{c'z + d'}\right) + b}{c\left(\frac{a'z + b'}{c'z + d'}\right) + d}\\
  & = \frac{a(a'z + b') + b(c'z+d')}{c(a'z + b') + d(c'z + d')}\\
  & = \frac{(aa'+bc')z + (ab'+bd')}{(ca'+dc')z + (cb'+dd')}\\
  & = \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} aa'+bc' & ab'+bd'\\ ca'+dc' & cb'+dd' \end{array}\right]\right)\\
  & = \psi\left(
\left[\begin{array}{cc} a & b\\ c & d \end{array}\right]
\left[\begin{array}{cc} a' & b'\\ c' & d' \end{array}\right]
\right)\\
\end{align}$$

which shows that $$\psi$$ is a homomorphism.

* b) The kernel of $$\psi$$ is 

$$\ker(\psi) = \left\lbrace
\left[\begin{array}{cc} a & b\\ c & d \end{array}\right]: \frac{az + b}{cz + d} = z
\right\rbrace.$$

If $$\frac{az + b}{cz + d} = z$$, then $$az+b = cz^2+dz$$.
Comparing like terms, this says $$c=b=0$$ and $$a=d$$.
Thus the kernel is

$$\ker(\psi) = \left\lbrace
\left[\begin{array}{cc} a & 0\\ 0 & a \end{array}\right]: a\in \mathbb C
\right\rbrace = N.$$

Thus by the First Isomorphism Theorem

$$\text{PGL}_2(\mathbb C) = \text{GL}_2(\mathbb C)/N \cong \text{img}(\psi) = \mathcal M.$$

* c)  Note that

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 3 & -1\\ -1 & 3 \end{array}\right] = 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 1 & 1 \end{array}\right] 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 4 \end{array}\right] 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 1 & 1 \end{array}\right]^{-1}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 3 & -1\\ -1 & 3 \end{array}\right]^{2025} = 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 1 & 1 \end{array}\right] 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 2^{2025} & 0\\ 0 & 4^{2025} \end{array}\right] 
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 1 & 1 \end{array}\right]^{-1} = 
2^{2024}
\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1+2^{2025} & 1-2^{2025}\\ 1-2^{2025} & 1+2^{2025} \end{array}\right].
$$

Consequently

$$
\begin{align}
\chi^{2025}(z) 
  & = \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} 3 & -1\\ -1 & 3 \end{array}\right]\right)^{2025} \\
  & = \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} 3 & -1\\ -1 & 3 \end{array}\right]^{2025}\right) \\
  & = \psi\left(\left[\begin{array}{cc} 1+2^{2025} & 1-2^{2025}\\ 1-2^{2025} & 1+2^{2025} \end{array}\right]\right)\\
  & = \frac{(1+2^{2025})z+1-2^{2025}}{(1-2^{2025})z+1+2^{2025}}.
\end{align}$$

Thus

$$\chi^{2025}(1) = \frac{(1+2^{2025})+1-2^{2025}}{(1-2^{2025})+1+2^{2025}} = \frac{2}{2}=1.$$

**Problem 5:**

In this problem, we will prove **Cayley's Theorem** that every finite group turns out to be isomorphic to a subgroup of a symmetric group.

Let $$G$$ be a group with $$n$$ elements

$$G = \{g_1,g_2,\dots, g_n\}.$$

For each fixed integer $$1\leq j\leq n$$, define a function

$$\sigma_j: \{1,2,\dots,n\}\rightarrow\{1,2,\dots,n\}$$

by letting $$\sigma_j(k)$$ be the unique integer with $$g_jg_k = g_{\sigma_j(k)}$$.

a) Prove that $$\sigma_j$$ is actually a bijection, and hence an element of $$S_n$$.
b) Prove that 

$$\sigma_i(\sigma_j(k)) = \sigma_{\sigma_i(j)}(k).$$

c) Prove that the function $$\varphi: G\rightarrow S_n$$ defined by

$$\varphi(g_j) = \sigma_j,\quad\text{for all}\ 1\leq j\leq n$$

is a monomorphism.
d) Use the **First Isomorphism Theorem** to explain why (c) implies $$G$$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $$S_n$$

**Solution:**

* a) To prove $$\sigma_j$$ is a bijection, it suffices to show that it is injective.  If $$\sigma_j(i) = \sigma_j(k)$$, then $$g_jg_k = g_jg_i$$.  Therefore $$g_k=g_i$$, so that $$k=i$$.  This proves injectivity and thus $$\sigma_j$$ is a bijection.

* b) Using associativity:

$$g_{\sigma_i(\sigma_j(k))} = g_ig_{\sigma_j(k)} = g_i(g_jg_k) = (g_ig_j)g_k = g_{\sigma_i(j)}g_k = g_{\sigma_{\sigma_i(j)}(k)}.$$

It follows that $$\sigma_i(\sigma_j(k)) = \sigma_{\sigma_i(j)}(k)$$.

Note that this implies that $$\sigma_i\sigma_j = \sigma_{\sigma_i(j)}$$

* c) From part (b), we know that

$$\varphi(g_ig_j) = \varphi(g_{\sigma_i(j)}) = \sigma_{\sigma_i(j)} = \sigma_i\sigma_j = \varphi(g_i)\varphi(g_j).$$

Therefore $$\varphi$$ is a group homomorphism.
If $$\varphi(g_i) = e$$, then $$\sigma_i=e$$ so that 

$$g_ig_j = g_{\sigma_i(j)} = g_{e(j)} = g_j.$$

This implies that $$g_i =e$$.  Hence $$\ker(\varphi) = \{e\}$$, forcing $$\varphi$$ to be a monomorphism.

* d) By the First Isomorphism Theorem

$$G\cong G/\{e\} = G/\ker(\varphi)\cong \text{img}(\varphi).$$

Since $$\text{img}(\varphi)$$ is a subgroup of $$S_n$$, this proves that $$G$$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $$S_n$$.
